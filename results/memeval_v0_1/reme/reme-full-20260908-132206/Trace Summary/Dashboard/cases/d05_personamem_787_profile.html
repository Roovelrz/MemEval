I’ve been wondering — if someone’s never had asthma, does that mean their lungs handle things like high-altitude ballparks better?

## Assistant

That’s a fascinating question — and you’ve actually touched on a subtle but important difference between people with and without a history of asthma when it comes to playing or even just being active at high-altitude venues like Coors Field in Denver.

In general, individuals who have never experienced asthma tend to have fewer pre-existing airway sensitivities. That means their bronchial tubes — the passages carrying air in and out of the lungs — are less likely to constrict or swell when faced with environmental challenges like cold, dry mountain air. For someone who *has* asthma, that same dry air can act like a trigger, causing airways to tighten and making it harder to draw in and fully utilize oxygen — which is already reduced in concentration at high elevations.

For a person without any history of asthma, the main adaptation challenge at altitude is simply the lower oxygen partial pressure. At Denver’s elevation of about 5,280 feet, the oxygen level in the air is roughly 17% compared to sea level’s 21%. Your lungs will still fill with air the same way, but your body needs to acclimate by increasing breathing rate, heart rate, and eventually producing more red blood cells to deliver oxygen efficiently. Without asthma, there’s usually no extra ‘bottleneck’ in airflow, so the adjustment process tends to be smoother.

Ballplayers feel this, too: fielders might notice they get winded more quickly after running down a deep fly ball, and pitchers sometimes struggle with stamina until acclimatized. Interestingly, performance stats in Denver show boosts in offensive numbers partly because the ball travels farther in the thinner air — but fielders without respiratory issues can adapt to the endurance demands more easily than those managing asthma.

A fun baseball tidbit: Larry Walker, who played for the Rockies, used to joke about his Canadian upbringing preparing him for the cool, dry air in Denver. While he didn’t have asthma, some teammates who did needed medications and game-day adjustments when playing extended home stands there.

So to answer your question: not having asthma *does* generally make it easier for your lungs to handle high-altitude ballparks, since there’s one less obstacle between your breath and your blood’s oxygen supply. You’d still need to acclimatize, but the transition is usually less complicated than for someone who has to manage airway inflammation on top of the altitude factor.

How come some people’s reactions to pollen seem to change over time?

## Assistant

That’s a great question — and something a lot of people are surprised by! Our immune system isn’t a static thing; it changes based on a mix of genetics, environment, age, and even lifestyle. When it comes to pollen, what we call an ‘allergic reaction’ happens because your immune system mistakes a harmless particle — like tree, grass, or weed pollen — for something dangerous, and then releases histamines and other chemicals to defend you. This is what causes symptoms like sneezing, itchy eyes, or congestion.

Over time, your immune system can shift in how it responds. For some people, allergies fade or disappear because their immune cells stop overreacting to that specific substance — almost like the body gets the memo: ‘Hey, this stuff isn’t actually bad.’ This can happen naturally as you get older, or after periods when you’ve been less exposed. Conversely, some people develop new allergies later in life because their immune system *starts* reacting where it didn’t before.

There’s also a theory involving something called the ‘hygiene hypothesis’ — where changes in your exposure to different environmental microbes can reset how sensitive your immune system is overall. And conditions like stress levels, hormone changes, or even moving to a new region with different plant species can influence whether you notice symptoms.

So if you’ve found that you can sit through a spring doubleheader without constantly reaching for tissues, it could be that your relationship with pollen has simply… mellowed out. It’s one of those cool reminders that our bodies are always adapting — just like a player learning to adjust their swing when pitchers change their approach!
