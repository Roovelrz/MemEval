How come some people’s reactions to pollen seem to change over time?

## Assistant

That’s a great question — and something a lot of people are surprised by! Our immune system isn’t a static thing; it changes based on a mix of genetics, environment, age, and even lifestyle. When it comes to pollen, what we call an ‘allergic reaction’ happens because your immune system mistakes a harmless particle — like tree, grass, or weed pollen — for something dangerous, and then releases histamines and other chemicals to defend you. This is what causes symptoms like sneezing, itchy eyes, or congestion.

Over time, your immune system can shift in how it responds. For some people, allergies fade or disappear because their immune cells stop overreacting to that specific substance — almost like the body gets the memo: ‘Hey, this stuff isn’t actually bad.’ This can happen naturally as you get older, or after periods when you’ve been less exposed. Conversely, some people develop new allergies later in life because their immune system *starts* reacting where it didn’t before.

There’s also a theory involving something called the ‘hygiene hypothesis’ — where changes in your exposure to different environmental microbes can reset how sensitive your immune system is overall. And conditions like stress levels, hormone changes, or even moving to a new region with different plant species can influence whether you notice symptoms.

So if you’ve found that you can sit through a spring doubleheader without constantly reaching for tissues, it could be that your relationship with pollen has simply… mellowed out. It’s one of those cool reminders that our bodies are always adapting — just like a player learning to adjust their swing when pitchers change their approach!
